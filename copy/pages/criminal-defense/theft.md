## An accusation is not the whole story

A theft charge can feel personal in a way other charges don't. It can sound like a judgment on your character, not just a claim about one afternoon.

It is an accusation, not a conviction. What happened, what you meant and what the evidence shows are all still open questions. Your defense starts with getting them answered.

## What to do before your first court date

- **Talk to a lawyer before you talk to anyone else.** That includes the store, your employer and the person who made the accusation.
- **Don't try to fix it on your own.** Offers to pay something back or to explain can be misunderstood. Ask Will first.
- **Keep your records.** Receipts, bank statements, texts and emails can show what really happened.
- **Write down names.** Who was there, who saw what, and who you spoke to afterward.

1. **The charge.** An arrest or a citation comes with a first court date. Bring every paper you were given.
2. **Your consultation.** You give your side. Will explains the charge and your options in plain terms. {fact:F099}
3. **The facts.** Your defense goes through the reports, any video, the witness statements and the paper trail, and checks whether the story holds together.
4. **Your options.** Will looks for every lawful way to get the charges reduced or dismissed. {fact:F150} Some cases are resolved without a trial. Others are decided in court.

## When the accusation comes from work

Some theft charges begin with an employer: missing cash, a shortfall in the books, or an audit that points at you. These cases can turn on records as much as on witnesses.

If that is your situation, gather what you can lawfully keep, such as your own pay stubs and messages. Don't take company files. Bring what you have to your consultation, and read the [fraud defense](/criminal-defense/fraud/) page for related charges.
